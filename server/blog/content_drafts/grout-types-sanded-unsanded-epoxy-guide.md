<h2>Sanded grout: the standard choice</h2>
<p>Sanded cement grout contains fine sand that adds strength and reduces shrinkage as it cures, which is why it's the standard choice for joints 1/8 inch or wider — most floor tile and many backsplash installs fall into this category. It should meet <a href="https://tcnatile.com/resource-center/ansi-standards/">ANSI A118.6</a> and be installed per ANSI A108.10 guidelines.</p>

<h2>Unsanded grout: for narrow joints and delicate surfaces</h2>
<p>Unsanded grout skips the sand for a smoother finish, and is the right choice for joints narrower than 1/8 inch — common with rectified or large-format tile set with tight, uniform joints (see our <a href="/blog/large-format-gauged-porcelain-tile-what-to-ask">large-format porcelain guide</a>). It's also the safer choice on polished marble, glass tile, or any surface that sand could scratch during application.</p>

<span data-media-marker="2" style="display:none"></span>

<h2>Epoxy grout: maximum stain and chemical resistance</h2>
<p>Epoxy grout meets ANSI A118.3, with water absorption under 0.1% and significantly higher abrasion resistance, bond strength, and compressive strength than cement grout. It costs more and is more technique-sensitive to install (it sets faster and is harder to clean off tile surfaces during application), which is why it's most often specified for commercial kitchens, high-traffic floors, and anywhere stain resistance genuinely matters — a busy family kitchen backsplash near a stove, for example.</p>

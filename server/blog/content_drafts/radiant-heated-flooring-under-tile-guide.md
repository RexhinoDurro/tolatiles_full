<h2>How it actually installs</h2>
<p>The most common residential system, <a href="https://www.schluter.com/schluter-us/en_US/Membranes/Uncoupling-(DITRA)/Schluter%C2%AE-DITRA-HEAT/p/DITRA_HEAT">Schluter-DITRA-HEAT</a>, combines an uncoupling membrane (the same crack-isolation function covered in our <a href="/blog/backer-board-guide-cement-board-vs-ditra">cement board vs. Ditra guide</a>) with a stud structure the heating cable snaps directly into — no clips, fasteners, or self-leveling compound needed to encapsulate the cable. That means adding radiant heat doesn't require a separate installation step beyond what a quality tile floor already needs.</p>

<h2>Tile requirements</h2>
<p>Tile set over a radiant heat membrane needs to be a minimum of roughly 2x2 inches and at least 5.5mm thick — which covers the overwhelming majority of ceramic and porcelain tile, so this rarely limits material choice in practice.</p>

<span data-media-marker="2" style="display:none"></span>

<h2>Is it worth it in a Florida climate?</h2>
<p>Florida's climate means radiant floor heat isn't solving a real cold-house problem the way it would in a northern state — it's a comfort feature. The most common reasoning homeowners give is simple: a tile floor feels cold underfoot year-round regardless of the thermostat, and radiant heat fixes that specifically, plus it covers the genuinely cool mornings Jacksonville and St. Augustine still get several weeks a year.</p>

<h2>Best time to add it</h2>
<p>Radiant heat is most cost-effective when added during a floor tile project that's already happening — the membrane and cable installation adds relatively little labor on top of a floor that's being torn out and retiled anyway, versus a standalone retrofit that requires removing an existing floor specifically for this purpose.</p>

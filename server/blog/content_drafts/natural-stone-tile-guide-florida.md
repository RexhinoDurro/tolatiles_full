<h2>Sealing is not optional</h2>
<p>Natural stone should be sealed prior to grouting, again once installation is complete, and periodically thereafter to maintain its appearance and resist staining. A penetrating sealer (impregnator) is the standard for most residential stone — it soaks into the stone's porous structure to repel liquids while letting the stone breathe, without changing how the stone looks the way a topical (surface) sealer can. Pre-sealing before grout is applied also helps prevent grout haze and staining from working into the stone during installation.</p>

<h2>Humidity considerations for Florida</h2>
<p>Florida's humidity doesn't damage sealed natural stone directly, but it does mean a compromised or overdue seal job shows problems faster — moisture that would bead off a properly sealed stone can work into an under-sealed one and contribute to staining or, in bathroom settings, create conditions for mildew at grout lines. This is part of why <a href="/blog/prevent-mold-mildew-florida-bathroom-guide">bathroom ventilation</a> matters just as much with stone as with any other tile.</p>

<span data-media-marker="2" style="display:none"></span>

<h2>Where natural stone earns its cost</h2>
<p>Natural stone runs $20 to $100+ per square foot installed — meaningfully more than porcelain — and the honest case for it is aesthetic: no manufactured tile fully replicates the depth and variation of real stone veining or texture. It earns that premium most clearly in a primary bathroom, an accent wall, or a lower-traffic space where the maintenance commitment is manageable and the visual payoff is highest.</p>

<h2>Large-format tile: fewer lines, more room</h2>
<p>Grout lines are the visual mechanism that makes a floor read as "made of pieces" rather than one continuous surface. A 12x24 or larger tile on a small bathroom floor cuts the number of grout lines dramatically compared to a standard 12x12, and the room reads more open with the exact same square footage. This does require a flatter subfloor than standard tile — see our guide to <a href="/guides/large-format-gauged-porcelain-tile-what-to-ask">large-format and gauged porcelain</a> for what that actually involves.</p>

<h2>Grout color: the overlooked lever</h2>
<p>A tile floor with dark, high-contrast grout creates a visible grid the eye traces across the room, which works against you in a small space. Grout that's closely matched to the tile color minimizes that grid effect, so the same tile can read noticeably more open just by changing the grout choice — a low-cost adjustment most homeowners never think to ask about.</p>

<span data-media-marker="2" style="display:none"></span>

<h2>Continuous tile into a curbless shower</h2>
<p>The single strongest small-bathroom move is running the same tile from the bathroom floor straight into the shower with no curb or threshold to break the plane. The floor visually extends into the shower rather than stopping at a physical barrier — see our guide on <a href="/guides/curbless-showers-aging-in-place-modern-luxury">curbless showers</a> for the structural prep this requires.</p>

<h2>Where bold pattern still fits</h2>
<p>None of this means a small bathroom has to be plain. A patterned accent wall, a single niche in hex or Zellige tile, or a bold shower floor all work in a small room — as long as the rest of the space stays simple. One focal point reads intentional; pattern everywhere reads cluttered no matter the room size.</p>

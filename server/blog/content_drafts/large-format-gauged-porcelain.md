<p><strong>TL;DR:</strong> Large-format tile (12x24 and up) and gauged porcelain slabs (up to 5x10 feet) create a seamless, high-end look, but they demand a flatter subfloor, more careful handling, and real experience to install without visible lippage or hollow spots. Ask any contractor for photos of large-format work before you hire them — not just their general portfolio.</p>

<p>Large-format porcelain is one of the biggest design trends in Northeast Florida right now, in kitchens, <a href="/st-augustine/services/floor-tile-installation">floors</a>, and showers alike — fewer grout lines, a cleaner look, and a floor that reads almost like a single continuous slab. It's also one of the easiest ways for an inexperienced installer to turn an expensive material into a visibly flawed floor. Here's what actually separates a good large-format install from a bad one.</p>

<span data-media-marker="1" style="display:none"></span>

<h2>What counts as large-format or gauged porcelain?</h2>
<p><strong>Large-format tile</strong> generally means anything with a side 15 inches or longer — 12x24, 24x24, and 24x48 are the most common sizes we install. <strong>Gauged porcelain panels</strong> (sometimes called porcelain slabs) go further still, up to roughly 5x10 feet in a single ultra-thin panel, often 6 to 12mm thick. Slabs that size are typically used for shower walls, kitchen islands, and statement floors where a truly seamless surface is the goal.</p>

<h2>Standard tile vs. large-format vs. gauged porcelain</h2>
<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Category</th><th>Typical size</th><th>Thickness</th><th>Subfloor flatness needed</th><th>Handling</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>Standard tile</td><td>Up to 12x12 or 13x13</td><td>~3/8 in</td><td>1/4 in over 10 ft</td><td>One installer</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Large-format tile</td><td>12x24 to 24x48</td><td>3/8–1/2 in</td><td>1/8 in over 10 ft</td><td>One installer, leveling clips recommended</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Gauged porcelain slab</td><td>Up to 5x10 ft</td><td>6–12mm</td><td>1/8 in over 10 ft, often tighter</td><td>Two installers minimum, suction cups</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>
<p>Flatness tolerances above follow the <a href="https://tcnatile.com/products/publications/2026-tcna-handbook-for-ceramic-glass-and-stone-tile-installation/">TCNA Handbook for Ceramic, Glass, and Stone Tile Installation</a>, the industry reference for substrate and installation standards.</p>

<h2>Why it's harder to install than it looks</h2>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Subfloor flatness.</strong> Standard tile can hide minor dips and humps in a subfloor. Large-format tile can't — the same imperfection that's invisible under a 12x12 tile shows up as a rocking tile or a visible lip along a 24-inch edge. Large-format work needs a subfloor leveled to a much tighter tolerance than most crews are used to holding.</li>
  <li><strong>Full mortar coverage.</strong> Large tiles need a much higher percentage of thinset coverage on the back of the tile (back-buttering, not just troweling the floor) to avoid hollow spots that crack under weight later.</li>
  <li><strong>Lippage control.</strong> Keeping every tile edge flush with its neighbor across a bigger surface usually requires a mechanical tile leveling system — clips and wedges that hold tiles flush while the mortar sets — rather than eyeballing it.</li>
  <li><strong>Handling.</strong> A single gauged porcelain slab can weigh well over 100 pounds and needs two installers with suction cups to move and set it without cracking. This isn't a one-person job.</li>
</ul>

<span data-media-marker="2" style="display:none"></span>

<h2>Questions to ask before you hire anyone for large-format tile</h2>
<ol>
  <li><strong>"Can I see photos of large-format or gauged porcelain jobs you've actually completed?"</strong> A general tile portfolio isn't the same skill set. Ask specifically.</li>
  <li><strong>"What subfloor flatness tolerance do you work to?"</strong> A real answer references an actual measurement, commonly 1/8 inch over 10 feet for large-format work, not just "we make sure it's level."</li>
  <li><strong>"Do you use a tile leveling system?"</strong> This should be a yes for tile 15 inches or larger.</li>
  <li><strong>"How many installers handle the slabs?"</strong> Two-person handling for anything oversized is a basic safety and quality standard, not an upsell.</li>
  <li><strong>"What's your lippage tolerance?"</strong> An experienced installer will have a specific number in mind, generally under 1/32 inch for large-format work.</li>
</ol>

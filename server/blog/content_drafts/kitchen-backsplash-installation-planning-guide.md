<h2>Full-height vs. partial-height</h2>
<p>Full-height backsplash — tile running from the countertop all the way to the underside of the upper cabinets — is the current standard, and it reads noticeably more finished than the partial-height backsplash (stopping a few inches above the counter) that was common in the 2000s and 2010s. Full-height does use more tile, but the visual payoff is significant, especially with a patterned or natural-stone tile.</p>

<h2>Outlet cutouts and switch plates</h2>
<p>Outlets almost never need to be relocated for a backsplash refresh. The standard approach removes the existing cover plates, tiles around the boxes, and reinstalls the covers flush against the new tile surface using outlet extender rings — a normal part of any backsplash install, not a special request. Outlets only need to move if you're changing the layout itself, like adding a pot filler or shifting where a range hood sits.</p>

<span data-media-marker="2" style="display:none"></span>

<h2>Realistic timeline</h2>
<p>Budget 2 to 3 days for a standard backsplash: one day to set the tile, a cure period (typically 24 hours) before grouting, and a final day to grout, clean, and seal if needed. Intricate patterns, natural stone requiring sealant, or a larger-than-average footprint can extend this — see our <a href="/blog/natural-stone-tile-guide-florida">natural stone tile guide</a> if stone is on your shortlist.</p>

<h2>Material shortlist</h2>
<p>Porcelain and ceramic subway tile remain the most common, low-risk backsplash choice, while zellige, natural stone, and glass tile all trend upward in both cost and visual impact — see our <a href="/blog/mediterranean-kitchen-tile-design-ideas-st-augustine">Mediterranean kitchen tile design ideas</a> and <a href="/blog/two-tone-kitchen-tile-design-ideas">two-tone kitchen tile ideas</a> for material and color inspiration.</p>
